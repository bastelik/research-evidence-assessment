# Claim Verification Method

Use this method when checking statements in reports, articles, translations, research summaries or AI-generated responses.

## Step 1 — Preserve the exact claim

Copy the statement exactly. Record who made it, where it appears and what decision depends on it. Do not quietly rewrite a claim to make it easier to verify.

## Step 2 — Decompose it

Split compound claims into independently testable parts. Preserve:
- Negation
- Modality (may, might, can, must, will)
- Quantifiers (some, most, all)
- Comparisons and baselines
- Dates and time periods
- Causal language
- Geographic and population scope

## Step 3 — Identify the evidence needed

Ask what source would actually establish the claim. A press release may establish that an organization announced a target; it may not establish that the target was achieved.

## Step 4 — Locate and inspect sources

Prefer direct, relevant and methodologically transparent evidence. Inspect the source itself, not only a search snippet or another writer's description. Record exact locations.

## Step 5 — Compare claim and source

For each claim component, record:
- Supported
- Partially supported
- Contradicted
- Not stated / not established
- Unclear

Do not treat “not stated” as proof that the claim is false. Do not treat a source's silence as confirmation.

## Step 6 — Check scope and qualifiers

Ask whether the source supports the same:
- People or population
- Place
- Period
- Measure or definition
- Degree of certainty
- Causal interpretation

A narrower finding cannot justify a broader statement without additional evidence.

## Step 7 — Write the conclusion

Use calibrated language and explain the limitation. If evidence is insufficient, say what additional source or data would resolve the question.

## Miniature synthetic example

**Claim:** “The pilot increased employment across the region.”

**Available source:** A fictional pilot report says 18 participants completed a job-readiness course. It reports no employment follow-up and no regional comparison.

**Assessment:**
- The report supports the claim that 18 participants completed the course.
- It does not establish that participants obtained jobs.
- It does not establish a regional employment increase.
- The claim is broader than the available evidence.

**Defensible wording:** “The pilot report records 18 course completions. The report does not provide employment follow-up or evidence of a region-wide employment effect.”

This example is synthetic and is intended to illustrate scope discipline, not to report a real program result.

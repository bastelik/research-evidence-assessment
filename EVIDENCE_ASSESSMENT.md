# Evidence Assessment Framework

Assess evidence in relation to a specific claim or question. Avoid assigning a source one universal reliability score without considering what it is being used to establish.

## 1. Define the claim

Break compound statements into atomic claims. Identify:
- The subject and action
- Time period and geographic scope
- Population or unit of analysis
- Quantity, comparison or causal relationship
- Conditions, qualifiers and degree of certainty

A claim such as “the program improved access and reduced costs for all participants” contains multiple propositions and should be assessed separately.

## 2. Evaluate the source for the claim

| Dimension | Questions |
|---|---|
| Authority | Who produced it? Are they qualified or institutionally responsible for this information? |
| Proximity | Is it a first-hand record, direct observation, analysis or later summary? |
| Method | How was the information collected and analyzed? |
| Transparency | Are sources, definitions, methods and limitations disclosed? |
| Currency | Is it current enough for this question? |
| Scope | Does the evidence cover the relevant population, location and period? |
| Independence | Is it independent, self-reported, commissioned or potentially conflicted? |
| Corroboration | Do independent sources support or challenge it? |

## 3. Determine the relationship between claim and evidence

Use these labels:
- **Supported:** evidence directly supports the claim within the stated scope.
- **Partially supported:** some elements are supported, but the claim is broader or stronger than the evidence.
- **Contradicted:** reliable evidence conflicts with the claim.
- **Not established:** the reviewed evidence does not establish the claim.
- **Unclear:** evidence is ambiguous, inaccessible or insufficient to decide.

“Not established” is not equivalent to “false.” State what was and was not checked.

## 4. Match confidence to evidence

Use calibrated language:
- Strong evidence: “The records show…” or “The study found…”
- Qualified evidence: “The available evidence suggests…”
- Limited evidence: “This may indicate…, but the evidence is limited.”
- No adequate support: “The reviewed sources do not establish…”

Do not use confident language merely because a source sounds authoritative.

## 5. Check common failure modes

- A finding about one site generalized to an entire country
- A short pilot presented as proof of long-term impact
- Activity counts presented as outcomes
- A correlation presented as causation
- A participant quotation presented as representative
- A dated source presented as current
- A secondary summary treated as direct evidence
- A missing result interpreted as a negative result
- A caveat, denominator or uncertainty interval omitted
- A claim about all participants based on a partial sample

## 7. Synthesize responsibly

When sources disagree:
1. Confirm that they address the same population, period and definition.
2. Compare methods and source proximity.
3. Check whether the disagreement is substantive or caused by different measures.
4. Report the divergence and explain which evidence is more relevant to the question.
5. Preserve unresolved uncertainty.

A defensible synthesis explains why a conclusion follows from the evidence and where it stops.
